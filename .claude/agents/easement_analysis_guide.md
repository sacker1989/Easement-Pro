### C. Professional Appraisal & Valuation Methodologies

These are the **industry-standard approaches** used by licensed appraisers to value easements:

1. **Before-and-After Method (Federal Rule)** — The most commonly used approach. The appraiser values the entire property *before* the easement, then values the remainder *after* the easement. The difference is the easement value.
2. **Value of Take Plus Damages (State Rule)** — Compensation = value of the land taken by the easement + damages to the remaining property.
3. **Sales Comparison Approach** — Comparing the subject property to similar properties with and without easements.
4. **Income Capitalization Approach** — Used when the easement generates ongoing revenue (e.g., pipeline rents, cell tower leases).
5. **Cost Approach** — Estimates replacement cost of improvements, less depreciation (rarely used for vacant land or conservation easements).

## Part 2: Valuation Metrics by Easement Type

### Utility Easements (Power, Gas, Fiber, Water)

| Metric | Typical Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Percentage of fee simple value** | 25%–75% of underlying land value | Varies by impact on allowed uses |
| **Per linear foot** | $5–$50/ft | For transmission corridors; higher in urban areas |
| **Per acre (taken)** | $15,000–$50,000/acre | Based on land value x percentage allocation |
| **Damage to remainder** | 0%–15% of total property value | Loss of development potential, proximity stigma |
| **Annual rent (if leased)** | $1,000–$15,000/year | Cell towers, pipelines; capitalized at 8%–12% |

**Example:** A 9-acre property valued at $280,000 had a 0.32-acre utility easement valued at 50% of fee simple value, resulting in a $5,000 easement valuation plus $2,200 in damages to the remainder.

### Drainage & Stormwater Easements

| Metric | Typical Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Land value of affected area** | 10%–40% of fee simple | Open channel swales vs. buried pipes |
| **Maintenance liability exposure** | $5,000–$25,000 | If owner fails to maintain and causes flooding |
| **Annual maintenance cost** | $300–$800/year | Vegetation control, debris removal |
| **Impact on buildable area** | Reduces lot coverage by 5%–15% | May trigger setback violations |

### Access Easements (Driveways, Shared Roads, Emergency Lanes)

| Metric | Typical Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Value impact** | 2%–8% of total property value | Depends on exclusivity and width |
| **Annual maintenance (shared)** | $400–$2,400/year | Gravel resurfacing, snow removal, marking |
| **Liability exposure** | $3,000–$10,000 | Trip hazards, obstruction claims |
| **Benefit to dominant estate** | +5%–15% value increase | The property gaining access often sees value rise |

### Conservation Easements

| Metric | Typical Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Value reduction** | 40%–70% of development value | Depends on restriction severity |
| **Tax deduction value** | Before value - After value | Must be donated to qualified organization |
| **Per-acre payment (purchase programs)** | $2,000–$10,000/acre | Federal (NRCS) and state purchase programs |
| **Stewardship cost (annual)** | $5–$50/acre | Monitoring, invasive species control |

### General Valuation Factors Across All Types

1. **Location within property** — Easements crossing prime areas cause greater value loss than those along boundaries.
2. **Percentage of property affected** — A 5% strip has less impact than a 30% corridor.
3. **Duration** — Permanent easements reduce value more than temporary (e.g., construction) easements.
4. **Exclusivity** — Exclusive easements (only the holder can use the area) cause more damage than non-exclusive.
5. **Future development potential** — If the easement blocks the highest and best use, the impact is maximized.

## Part 3: Homeowner's Guide — How to Read a Recorded Deed and Plat

### What Is a Plat Map?

A **plat map** is a scaled, legally recorded diagram showing how land is divided into lots, blocks, streets, and easements within a subdivision. It is created by a licensed land surveyor, approved by local government, and recorded at the county courthouse. Think of it as the official blueprint of your neighborhood's layout.

### How to Find Your Plat Map

1. **County Recorder or Register of Deeds** — Search online using your property address or owner's name.
2. **Your deed** — The legal description references the plat book and page number.
3. **County GIS portal** — Most counties offer free online parcel viewers with plat overlays.
4. **Title company** — Check your closing documents from when you purchased the home.

---

### How to Read a Plat Map: Step-by-Step

#### Step 1: Check the Title Block
Located in a corner, it contains:
- Subdivision name (e.g., "Maplewood Estates, Unit 3")
- County, city, and state
- Date prepared and recorded
- Surveyor's name and license number
- **Scale** (e.g., 1" = 50') — *Always verify this first*

#### Step 2: Find Your Lot and Block
- **Blocks** are larger groupings (labeled A, B, 1, 2, etc.)
- **Lots** are individual parcels within blocks
- Your legal description reads: *"Lot 14, Block 3, Maplewood Estates, Unit 3, as recorded in Plat Book 22, Page 47"*

#### Step 3: Read Lot Lines and Dimensions
- Solid lines define your boundaries
- Numbers along lines show length in feet (e.g., "75.00'")
- **Front lot line** faces the street
- **Rear lot line** is opposite the street
- **Side lot lines** separate you from neighbors

#### Step 4: Understand Bearings (Direction Notations)
Bearings look like: **N 45°30'15" E**
- This means the line runs 45 degrees, 30 minutes, 15 seconds **east of due north**
- You don't need to calculate these, but know they define the precise legal direction of each boundary

#### Step 5: Identify Easements (The Critical Step)
- Easements are shown as **dashed lines** with labels
- Look for notations like: *"15' Utility Easement"* or *"10' Drainage Easement"*
- Common symbols: dashed lines, shaded strips, or notation boxes
- **Trace every dashed line on your lot** — this is where most homeowners make costly mistakes

#### Step 6: Note the Legend
The legend explains symbols for:
- Iron pins (property corner markers)
- Monuments (reference points)
- Centerlines (roads, utilities)
- Building setback lines

#### Step 7: Look for Written Notes and Restrictions
Many plats include a **notes section** with legally binding restrictions:
- Minimum house sizes
- Architectural review requirements
- Prohibited uses
- HOA obligations

### How to Read a Recorded Deed

A **deed** is the legal document that transfers ownership. It contains the **legal description** — the precise written definition of your property boundaries.

#### Key Sections of a Deed

**1. Grantor and Grantee**
- **Grantor** = Seller (person transferring the property)
- **Grantee** = Buyer (person receiving the property)

**2. Legal Description**
This is NOT your street address. It is one of two formats:

**A. Lot and Block (most common in subdivisions):**
> "Lot 14, Block 3, Maplewood Estates, Unit 3, according to the plat thereof recorded in Plat Book 22, Page 47, of the Public Records of [County], [State]."

**B. Metes and Bounds (common in rural areas):**
> "Beginning at the intersection of the North line of Section 12 and the East line of Oak Street; thence N 45° E 200 feet; thence S 45° E 150 feet; thence S 45° W 200 feet; thence N 45° W 150 feet to the Point of Beginning."

**3. Exceptions and Reservations**
This section lists **easements and encumbrances** that remain on the property:
> "Subject to a 15-foot utility easement along the North boundary, as recorded in Book 882, Page 442, and subject to all easements, covenants, and restrictions of record."

**4. Reference to Easement Documents**
Deeds often reference other recorded documents:
- Book and page numbers
- Instrument numbers
- Recording dates

Use these references to pull the **actual easement document** from the county recorder for full terms.

---

### Common Mistakes When Reading Plats and Deeds

| Mistake | Why It Matters | How to Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| **Confusing ROW with property line** | You may build on public land | The property line is set back from the street edge |
| **Ignoring easements** | You can't build in easement areas | Trace every dashed line before planning construction |
| **Using wrong scale** | Measurements will be off | Verify the scale notation; don't measure with a ruler on a copied page |
| **Assuming plat = current conditions** | Fences may be in wrong places | Get a current survey for boundary disputes |
| **Overlooking notes** | Hidden restrictions can block your project | Read the entire notes section on the plat |
| **Missing building setback lines** | These are NOT property lines | Setback lines are inside the lot lines; building must stay behind them |
